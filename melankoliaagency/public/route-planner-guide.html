Section A: What the Route Planner is

An all-in-one workspace that bridges geographic viability and financial success without juggling maps, spreadsheets, email and a CRM.

At its core the planner is map-first. As you define the artist’s constraints, including travel preferences, party size and gear weight, it visualizes realistic transit paths across actual roadways, warning you of impossible drives or budget-draining detours before you ever send a pitch.

Combined with a granular outreach board, you can pitch multiple venues in parallel for any date, keep backup holds active, and generate agency-branded, personalized inquiry emails in one click. Whether it’s an intimate club run or a multi-bus campaign, this is your mission control.

Section B: Step-by-step guide to booking a Sacred Skin US West Coast run

The scenario. Sacred Skin is a three-member synthwave/new wave band on the Melankolia roster, travelling by ground with an estimated 80 kg of gear and seeking a guarantee-versus-door deal. We’ll route a five-show August 2026 run from Los Angeles to Seattle, built around a Friday-night anchor festival in San Francisco.
1

Open the Tour Library & start a project

Log in to the Melankolia Ops Platform and open Route Planner from the sidebar. The left Tour Library panel is your archive of active, draft and archived runs. Click Create New Tour Plan or New Plan to open a fresh configuration workspace.

In depth

The Tour Library is more than a file list. Every card shows the artist, region, date range and current status. Three actions live on each card:

  • Open: loads the full saved workspace: map, legs, pipeline, and every Outreach Board exactly where you left them.
  • Duplicate: clones an entire past route (stops, deal structure, even backup venues) as a starting template. This is the fastest way to re-route a similar region for a different artist or a repeat run.
  • Delete: archives the tour. Its linked advancing show records are archived alongside it, not orphaned.

Create New Tour Plan opens a blank Route Builder form on the right rail. Nothing is written to the database until you generate the route. A half-filled builder is not saved as a tour.

Tip: If you’re booking a near-identical region you’ve routed before (say, last year’s West Coast run for a different band), duplicate a past tour instead of starting from scratch. You inherit the venue list and just re-date it.
2

Configure the Route Builder form

In the Route Builder on the right rail, enter Sacred Skin’s parameters: artist Sacred Skin, region US West Coast, dates in August 2026, five shows, start Los Angeles, end Seattle, a travel party of 3, approximately 80 kg of gear, ground travel, and the guarantee vs door deal type.

In depth

Every field in the builder feeds a different downstream system, so it’s worth knowing what each one actually drives:

  • Artist: is selected from your roster and pulls genre/market data used to match compatible venues later.
  • Region: scopes which cities and venues the AI considers when generating stops. A tighter region (e.g. "Pacific Northwest" vs "US West Coast") produces a denser, shorter run.
  • Dates & show count: the engine spaces stops across the window, targeting your requested number of shows without back-to-back grueling drives where avoidable.
  • Start / end city: anchors the geographic shape of the whole route; everything else routes between these two points.
  • Travel mode, party size, gear weight: these are the exact inputs the Transport Optimizer (Step 6) reuses, so fill them in accurately now to save re-entry later.
  • Deal type preference: guarantee / door split / versus: informs the target-rate suggestions you’ll see per stop and the language Estimate Budget uses.

Review these inputs before generating. Generate Route creates and autosaves a draft tour, so later changes should be made in the saved workspace rather than by assuming regeneration will preserve negotiated stops.

3

Establish your anchor shows

Lock your non-negotiable dates first so the engine routes around them. In Anchor Shows, add the Friday Aug 14, 2026 slot at San Francisco Gothic Fest (Grand Ballroom) with its guarantee, marked Confirmed.

In depth

An anchor is a date the AI treats as gravity, not a suggestion: the engine will never reassign, move, or overwrite an anchor slot when it generates or regenerates the route. Everything else gets built around it.

  • You can add multiple anchors to one tour: for example, a Friday festival plus an already-locked radio session two days later. The engine routes the drive-in and drive-out around both.
  • The Confirmed vs Hold marker is not cosmetic: it controls the anchor’s booking state and pipeline position. Projected Revenue uses the best available amount for each stop, in this order: confirmed rate, offer, target rate, or suggested guarantee.
  • Anchors typically carry the highest guarantee in the run: use them to set the financial floor, then let the rest of the route fill in around profitability.
Tip: Add anchors before you click Generate Route, not after: the routing engine uses them as fixed points when calculating the whole path, so anchoring first produces a tighter, more realistic itinerary.
4

Generate the AI route leg list

Click Generate Route. The engine uses route constraints, mapping data and drive guidelines to output a day-by-day itinerary: Los Angeles → San Francisco (anchor) → Sacramento → Portland → Seattle. Each stop lands in the Booking Pipeline board (Prospects → Holds → Negotiating → Confirmed) with its target rate and projected net.

In depth

Generate Route runs several passes at once: it checks live highway feasibility between candidate cities, cross-references your CRM’s venue history for genre/market fit, and applies spacing logic so you’re not routed into three 8-hour drives in a row without a rest day.

The Booking Pipeline board that appears is a live Kanban, not a static summary:

  • Prospects: stops the engine suggested but you haven’t reached out on yet.
  • Holds / Challenges: a venue has offered a hold, or there’s a routing conflict to resolve.
  • Negotiating: active back-and-forth on rate/deal terms.
  • Confirmed: the stop is locked in the Confirmed column. Revenue projections still use the best available rate on every stop, not only confirmed dates.

Drag any card between columns and it autosaves to Firestore instantly; there’s no separate save step for pipeline moves. The metrics row above the board (Shows / Days / Est. KM / Drive Hrs, then Currency / Projected Revenue / Fuel / Lodging / Per Diems / Projected Net) recalculates live as stops move and rates change. Fuel, Lodging and Per Diems are editable estimates. Adjust them to sharpen your Projected Net before you pitch.

Tip: Once outreach has started, adjust the saved tour with Stop Detail, AI Oversight or the inline Fix flow. Do not assume a fresh Generate Route pass will preserve active negotiations.
5

Analyze real driving paths & transit times

The map now draws real, highway-specific driving paths between stops. Each leg shows the true metrics: LA→SF (~380 mi, ~6 h), SF→Sacramento (~90 mi, ~1.5 h), Sacramento→Portland (~580 mi, ~9 h), Portland→Seattle (~175 mi, ~3 h). Flights render as dashed lines.

In depth

This isn’t a straight-line estimate between pins: the map calls a real routing engine (Google Routes API under the hood) to draw the actual highway path and return true drive time and distance for every ground leg. That’s why you see specific numbers like 580 mi / 9 h instead of a rounded guess.

  • Solid lines = driving legs, labeled with live drive-time and distance.
  • Dashed lines = flight legs (no road path to draw).

Use this view as your sanity check before locking anything in: a 9-hour overnight-adjacent drive is exactly the kind of thing that looks fine on a spreadsheet but breaks a crew in real life. If a leg looks brutal, that’s your cue to either add an off-day (Step 7) or re-open the Transport Optimizer (Step 6) to see if flying that leg is actually cheaper once you factor in an extra hotel night and drive-day per diems.

Tip: Real drive times also feed the Transport Optimizer’s dead-mile math directly, so there’s no need to re-enter mileage; it reads the same route data.
6

Compare transport: drive, one-way van, or fly

Before you commit to ground travel, open Transport & gear feasibility in the builder. Set the ground vehicle (rented or band-owned) and rental shape (return to origin or one-way drop). If the gear is flight-ready, tick Gear is flight-ready and add each individual flight case with its real weight.

Click Compare transport options. The planner compares van round-trip, one-way van plus a flight home, and flying the whole tour, then recommends the cheapest viable option. It uses available live pricing and identifies when fallback estimates are used, so the result can change between runs. Any case over 70 lb blocks the flight-only scenario and requires freight or ground transport.

In depth

This tool answers “should we drive this whole thing, or is flying actually cheaper?” using real calculations and current pricing inputs, not a rule of thumb.

  • Ground vehicle: rented vs owned: if the band owns the van, rental-cost scenarios drop out entirely; you’re only comparing fuel/dead-miles against flying.
  • Rental shape matters more than people expect: a return-to-origin rental means the van (and someone to drive it) has to go all the way back to the start city after the last show: that’s extra rental days, extra fuel, and extra crew time for miles that earn nothing. A one-way drop avoids all of that but usually carries a drop fee from the rental company.
  • Weekly vs daily rate is baked into the math automatically: a 7-day rental is priced at the weekly rate (roughly 4.6× daily, not 7×), so longer tours favor renting over owning or flying more than you’d guess.
  • Gear & flight rules (Adrian’s house rules, hard-coded): any single case over 50 lb triggers a heavy-bag fee (~$150); additional oversized cases are ~$40 each; any case over 70 lb hits the airline hard cap and blocks flying entirely for that piece; the tool marks fly-only infeasible and tells you to ship freight or drive instead.

The three scenarios it prices: van round-trip, one-way van + fly crew home, and fly the whole tour: use live flight fares and rental rates pulled via grounded web search (or real-time Amadeus pricing if that’s connected), not static averages. It ranks every feasible option by total cost and marks the cheapest one Recommended, with a plain-English reason (e.g. “dragging the van back 1,150 miles costs more than flying home”).

Tip: Re-run this any time party size, gear list, or the route’s start/end cities change: the recommendation can flip, especially once a route gets long enough that weekly rental pricing kicks in.
7

Review with AI Oversight & the Fix chat

The Sacramento→Portland leg is a grueling 9-hour drive. Click AI Oversight to scan for risks. It flags the long leg. Use the inline Ask AI / Propose Fix box: type “add a travel/off-day in Ashland, OR between Sacramento and Portland”, review the proposed change summary, and click Apply.

In depth

AI Oversight scans the whole generated route for risk patterns: not just long drives, but thin margins, day-of-week mismatches (a Tuesday stop in a market that only draws on weekends), and missing rest days between back-to-back long legs.

The inline Ask AI / Propose Fix box is deliberately a two-step flow, not an instant edit:

  • You describe the change in plain English (e.g. “add an off-day in Ashland between Sacramento and Portland”).
  • The AI returns a plain-English summary of exactly what will change, including the stop, date and field, before anything is written.
  • You review that diff, then click Apply to commit it or Dismiss to discard it. Nothing is auto-applied.
Tip: Use this for anything you’d otherwise do by hand in the leg list, including adding an off-day, nudging a date, or flagging a stop as high-risk. It’s faster than manually editing the leg row and it leaves you a paper trail of what changed and why.
8

Expand stop details & the Outreach Board

Click the Portland stop in the leg list to open the Stop Detail pane and its nested Outreach Board. Use Fast Contacts for immediate CRM matches or Contact Finder for a live web search, then review each synthwave/new wave-compatible room before adding it to outreach.

In depth

Clicking any stop in the leg list expands the Stop Detail pane: the date, market size, target rate, and any notes you’ve logged for that city. Nested inside is the Outreach Board for that single stop.

Contact suggestions can come from the internal venue CRM, live Contact Finder research, or manual additions. Review genre compatibility, room capacity and contact quality before selecting a venue for the stop.

Tip: If none of the pre-pulled venues feel right, you can add one manually from the Contact Manager and it’ll show up here for this stop: the board isn’t limited to auto-suggestions.
9

Manage parallel outreach & keep backups

Pitch several Portland venues in parallel and move each through To contact, Contacted, Awaiting reply, and Interested / talking as the conversation develops. When one venue is locked, use Confirm this venue. The remaining candidates keep their current outreach states and are grouped as backups automatically.

In depth

The Outreach Board’s status flow is built around real booking behavior: you rarely pitch just one room per date, and rooms fall through constantly, so the system is designed for parallel conversations from the start.

  • Working statuses are To contact, Contacted, Awaiting reply, Interested / talking, and Declined / passed. Confirmed and Fell through are set with their dedicated actions.
  • Only one venue per stop can be Confirmed at a time. Confirming one does not delete or rewrite the other candidates; the board groups them as backups while preserving their existing outreach information.
  • If a confirmed venue later falls through, promote the strongest backup with one click. Its full contact history, prior outreach notes, and any rate discussion are still attached, so you’re not starting that conversation over.
Tip: Keep at least one backup live even after confirming, especially for markets with few compatible rooms. It costs nothing to leave a backup warm, and it saves the whole leg if a confirmed date cancels close to the tour.
10

Monitor the tour-wide Outreach Board

Open the tour-wide Outreach Board from the saved-tour toolbar. Its summaries separate city coverage from venue activity, including confirmed cities, open cities, venues to contact, conversations and follow-ups due. Use Draft all follow-ups for contacts with no confirmation after seven or more days, and review hold-deadline alerts above the metrics.

In depth

This is the tour-wide command center. While the per-stop board (Step 9) manages one city, this view rolls every stop into one pipeline so nothing quietly stalls.

  • The KPI strip gives an at-a-glance count of confirmed stops, stops still in active conversation, and how many contacts are overdue for a follow-up.
  • Draft all follow-ups batch-generates warm, on-brand nudge emails (using the same Melankolia booking voice/style guide as the Email Generator) for every contact with no reply in 7+ days. You still review and send each one individually: nothing goes out automatically.
  • Hold-deadline alerts surface any venue hold that’s about to expire, so you don’t lose a date to another act simply because a deadline slipped past unnoticed.
Tip: Check this board at the start of your booking day, not just when something reminds you to. It’s built to be the first place you look, not a report you have to remember to run.
11

Review autosave & sync the tour

Generate Route already autosaves the draft tour and creates draft show records for Advancing. After further edits, use Save / Sync Tour as an explicit synchronization checkpoint before returning to the Tour Library.

In depth

On successful generation, the new tour receives an API-generated ID, appears in the Tour Library, and creates one draft Advancing record for each non-rest-day stop. Pipeline moves, stop edits, outreach changes and assistant-applied patches then persist against that saved tour.

Save / Sync Tour writes the latest generated-tour state and replaces its linked show records so the routing workspace and Advancing stay aligned.

Tip: Once the tour appears in the library, it can be duplicated as a template. For a future regional run, clone it and review every date, venue, rate and transport assumption before syncing.

Section D: Glossary of touring & booking terms

A shared vocabulary for deals, show-day production, holds, and travel/legal admin.

Deal & Financial Terms

Guarantee
A fixed, pre-negotiated fee that the promoter or venue guarantees to pay the artist for their performance, regardless of ticket sales or show attendance.
Door Deal
A financial arrangement where the artist does not receive a guaranteed fee, but is instead paid a set percentage of all ticket revenues collected at the entrance.
Door Split
A type of door deal where the gross or net box office receipts are split between the venue and the artist at a specified percentage, such as a 70/30 split in favor of the artist.
Versus (Vs) Deal
A deal structure where the artist receives either their flat guarantee or an agreed percentage of the ticket revenue, whichever amount is higher at the end of the night.
Backend
The financial revenues or percentages earned by an artist after the show's production expenses have been fully recouped and the initial guarantee has been covered.
Plus Deal
A financial structure where the artist receives a flat guarantee plus a percentage of ticket sales after the promoter's expenses and a pre-negotiated promoter profit are deducted.
Breakeven
The exact point at which ticket revenues match the total show production expenses (the "nut") and artist guarantees, resulting in zero net loss or profit.
Settlement
The meeting at the end of a show night between the tour manager and the promoter or venue representative to calculate final ticket sales, deduct expenses, and pay the artist.
Guarantee vs Percentage
A deal type identical to a Versus Deal, where the artist is paid either their flat guarantee or a set percentage of sales, depending on which is larger.
Deposit
An upfront payment (typically 10% to 50% of the guarantee) paid by the promoter to the booking agency before the show date to secure the booking.
Net vs Gross
Gross refers to the total revenue earned from ticket sales before any deductions; Net refers to the remaining revenue after taxes, credit card fees, and venue expenses are deducted.
Capacity
The maximum number of patrons legally allowed inside a venue, which determines the potential gross box office revenue.
Sold Ticket Count / Walk-Up
Sold ticket count is the total number of tickets purchased in advance or at the door; walk-up refers specifically to tickets purchased at the box office on the night of the show.
Comp / Comps
Short for complimentary tickets; these are free tickets allocated to the artist, venue, press, or promotional partners that do not generate box office revenue.
Nut (The Nut)
The total operating expenses of putting on a show (including venue staff, sound/lights, security, and marketing) that must be recouped before net splits are paid.
Merch Cut / Merch Split
The percentage of merchandise revenue that a venue retains (often 10-20% for soft goods like shirts, and 10% for hard goods like CDs/vinyl) for allowing the artist to sell on-site.
Ticket Scaling
The practice of pricing tickets differently based on seat location, demand, or purchasing tiers (e.g., General Admission vs. VIP).
GBOR (Gross Box Office Receipts)
The total money collected from all ticket sales for a show before any expenses, taxes, or facility fees are deducted.
NBOR (Net Box Office Receipts)
The ticket revenues remaining after mandatory deductions like sales tax, facility fees, and credit card processing fees have been subtracted from the GBOR.
Buyout
A cash payment given to the tour crew in lieu of providing catering, meals, or hotel rooms directly.
Per Diem
A daily cash allowance given to touring band and crew members to cover individual food and personal expenses while on the road.
One-Way Drop Fee
A surcharge a rental company adds when a van is picked up in one city and returned in another instead of back at the origin. This can be cheaper overall than paying extra days and fuel to dead-drive the vehicle home.
Dead Miles
Distance driven with no show attached to it, typically the empty return leg back to the tour’s origin. A key cost the Transport Optimizer weighs when deciding between a round-trip van and a one-way drop or flights.

Show-Day & Production Terms

Load-In
The scheduled time when the tour crew arrives at the venue to unload instruments, gear, and production equipment from their vehicles.
Load-Out
The process of packing up and reloading the band's gear and production equipment back into the vehicles after the performance concludes.
Soundcheck
The pre-show rehearsal period where the band and sound engineers test instruments and vocal microphones to balance the audio levels in the room and monitors.
Backline
The heavy musical equipment (such as drum kits, guitar amplifiers, and keyboard stands) provided by the venue or rented locally so the band doesn't have to transport them.
Rider (Hospitality vs Technical)
A technical rider specifies the exact audio, lighting, and stage requirements for the performance; a hospitality rider details the food, drinks, and dressing room requests for the artist and crew.
Day Sheet
A daily itinerary distributed to the tour party outlining the schedule, venue address, load-in times, contact info, hotel details, and curfew for that specific day.
Green Room
A private dressing room provided by the venue for the artists to relax, prepare, and eat before and after their performance.
In-House PA
The permanent sound reinforcement system (Public Address system) owned and operated by the venue, including main speakers, subwoofers, and monitors.

Hold & Booking Status Terms

Hold
A reservation placed by an agent on a specific date at a venue while they attempt to build or finalize the surrounding tour dates.
Challenge (on a Hold)
A process where a second agent tries to book a date that is already held; the first holding agent is given a strict deadline (usually 24-48 hours) to either confirm the date or release it.
First / Second Hold
A ranking system for date reservations; a first hold has the primary right of refusal, while a second hold will get the slot if the first hold is released or loses a challenge.
Anchor Date
A high-profile, high-paying, or locked-in date (such as a festival appearance) that forms the structural foundation of a tour itinerary around which other shows are routed.
Routing
The geographic sequence of show dates that minimizes travel time, distance, and expenses for a touring artist.
Off Day / Rest Day
A non-performance day in the tour schedule dedicated to travel, equipment maintenance, or resting the band and crew.

Travel, Legal & Admin Terms

Advance / Advancing
The process where the tour manager contacts the venue's production manager 1-2 weeks before the show to finalize schedules, technical specs, security, and hospitality details.
Radius Clause
A contractual restriction that prevents an artist from performing within a certain physical distance (e.g., 50 miles) of the venue for a specified time before and after the show.
Promoter
The individual or organization responsible for financial underwriting, local marketing, and overall execution of a live concert event.
Buyer
A professional who licenses or contracts talent for events, venues, or festivals (interchangeable with talent buyer).
Talent Buyer
An executive hired by a venue or promotion company to curate events, negotiate performance contracts, and book musical acts.
On-Sale
The official date and time when tickets are made available for purchase by the general public.
Drive Time / Drive Nut
Drive time is the actual duration spent traveling between cities; drive nut refers to the calculated cost of vehicle wear, gas, toll fees, and driver hours required to complete a leg.
Immigration / Visa (P-2 / O-1 for US)
Legal authorizations required for non-US artists to perform in America; a P-2 is for exchange artists, and an O-1 is for individuals of extraordinary ability.
Carnet
An international customs document (often called a merchandise passport) that allows touring artists to temporarily import and export musical instruments and gear without paying duties or taxes.
VAT / Withholding Tax
Value-Added Tax is a consumption tax added to tickets/goods in foreign territories; withholding tax is a percentage of the artist's performance fee withheld by the local government for tax compliance.
